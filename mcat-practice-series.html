MCAT Practice Series

Commercial MCAT preparation can be prohibitively expensive for college students. When two SI Supervisors proposed a more accessible, peer-led alternative, I began developing their idea into a semester-long academic support program.

The proposed 28-session series would be facilitated by experienced SI Supervisors who have performed exceptionally well on the MCAT, with an emphasis on question analysis, critical reasoning, and metacognitive problem-solving rather than traditional content review.

The initiative has two primary objectives: expand access to MCAT preparation and increase Sanger Learning Center utilization, with a projected goal of reaching at least 200 individual students per week. By reallocating existing paid staff hours, the program aims to expand services without increasing student staffing expenditures while demonstrating the Center's value to the university and strengthening opportunities for future funding.

Proposal abstract

Two SI Supervisors who have taken the MCAT and performed well proposed a recurring, peer-facilitated practice series for UT Austin students preparing for the exam. The program is designed to address a gap between independently available practice resources and the high cost of sustained, guided preparation.

The MCAT Practice Series provides two two-hour sessions each week over 14 instructional weeks, rotating among the four MCAT sections. Sessions concentrate on passage interpretation, experimental reasoning, quantitative setup, answer-choice evaluation, and metacognitive error analysis.

The operating model uses two existing SI Supervisors. Each allocates one two-hour meeting and one hour of preparation weekly from a current 10-hour paid appointment, while the program coordinator takes on or streamlines selected supervisor responsibilities. The proposal serves two purposes: making structured MCAT question-analysis support more accessible to students who cannot readily afford commercial preparation, and documenting the student reach and operational value achievable with existing staffing resources.

Selected design and management decisions

Decision 1

Teach question analysis rather than deliver content lectures.

Rationale. The two facilitators bring prior MCAT experience; the institutional advantage is guided reasoning practice, not an attempt to replicate commercial multi-subject content courses.

Implementation. Each session begins with an independent attempt, proceeds through collaborative deconstruction, and ends with an error diagnosis and transfer exercise.

Decision 2

Reallocate existing paid appointments.

Rationale. A permanent additional payroll commitment would create a barrier to launching a pilot.

Implementation. Three of each supervisor's ten paid weekly hours are designated for MCAT work, contingent on the coordinator's ability to take on or simplify corresponding duties. Both the supervisor-hours allocation and coordinator workload are recorded.

Decision 3

Plan attendance separately from room capacity.

Rationale. A lecture hall's seat count does not establish what one facilitator can support in an interactive session.

Implementation. Registration, waitlists, and a facilitation cap are reviewed alongside room layout and demand before capacity increases.

Decision 4

Design for a continuation decision, not an automatic expansion.

Rationale. A program can attract interest without being operationally sustainable.

Implementation. A midsemester checkpoint and end-of-semester review examine attendance, usefulness, staffing effort, and the options to continue, revise, expand, or stop the series.

The opportunity: access to guided preparation

02 / 09

Free MCAT preparation resources exist, including AAMC practice exams and publicly available study materials. Guided courses, structured tutoring, and commercial preparation packages can nevertheless require substantial out-of-pocket spending. For students with limited disposable income, those costs may restrict access to consistent instruction and feedback.

The series addresses one part of that problem: how students analyze difficult passages and multiple-choice questions. It does not seek to replace a complete MCAT content-review course. Instead, experienced peer facilitators help students interpret question stems, evaluate evidence, identify misleading answer choices, and examine their own reasoning.

Rollout pain points

A sliding-scale fee is the hardest part of this program to launch. These are the problems it would have to solve first.

Approval to charge a fee

A university unit usually needs sign-off before it charges students. A new fee takes time to approve and may not be ready by Spring 2027.

Confirming aid eligibility

Tying the free tier to financial aid means coordinating with the financial aid office and getting approval to confirm a student's status, without Sanger seeing any financial records. Self-reporting is simpler but harder to check.

Students outside federal aid

Some students who cannot afford the fee may not qualify for aid, including international students and students whose aid status is still pending. The sliding scale needs a path for them.

A semester fee in a drop-in program

Students attend by subject and RSVP one session at a time. A semester fee assumes semester enrollment, so paying students would need a separate registration path, and the refund rules would need to cover students who stop coming.

Collecting payment

Payments and refunds need a university-approved payment system and an account to receive the funds.

Deterring the students it is meant for

Any mention of a fee can keep away students who would qualify for the free tier. Promotion would need to lead with the free option and make the waiver easy to claim.

Coordinator workload

Administering tiers, waivers, payments, and refunds adds work to the coordinator role, which is already a condition of the staffing plan.

Unknown revenue

Revenue depends on how many students pay the full rate, which is unknown until registration opens. The budget cannot rely on fee income in the first term.

If the fee model is not approved before registration opens, the term runs with no fee and the waiver system is tested in a later semester.

The institutional opportunity

The proposal also examines whether an existing learning-support operation can reach additional students without adding student-employee payroll hours. The participating supervisors each lead one two-hour session per week and spend one paid hour preparing. As coordinator, I take on or streamline selected supervisory duties so those three hours are protected within existing appointments.

This is a redistribution of work, not the removal of its cost. The model includes the value of existing staff hours and identifies coordinator capacity as a condition of implementation. Its success is assessed through student participation, usefulness, session quality, workload sustainability, and the evidence available to support future investment.

Learning model

04 / 09

The learning model is deliberately question-first. Students attempt a problem individually before discussing their reasoning. Facilitators then guide the group through passage interpretation, evidence, calculations or experimental logic, answer-choice analysis, and transfer to a different question. Short explanations of underlying content are used only when necessary to resolve a problem-solving difficulty.

Each session contains three to five substantial practice questions, with the exact number determined by question complexity. The priority is meaningful analysis rather than completing the largest possible question set.

Sessions follow the Supplemental Instruction model. After an icebreaker and a look at the lesson objectives, students attempt each question on their own, then work in strategically formed pairs or small groups to explain how they reached their answers, then bring that reasoning to a whole-class discussion of the correct answer and the wrong answers students most often choose.

Demand, capacity, and outreach

06 / 09

The program targets 200 students per week across its two weekly sessions, with a planning range of 150 at the low end and 250 at the high end. These figures are planning assumptions, to be revised after the interest survey and the first three weeks of registration and attendance.

At the target, each session averages 100 attendees, which fills the 100-seat cap. The low end leaves a quarter of the seats open. The high end runs about 25 students per session over the cap, which would call for a waitlist, an additional facilitator, a third weekly session, or another approved capacity solution. Demand will not spread evenly across subjects, so registration is tracked session by session.

Student handout · passage and questions

Original practice passage:

Public libraries have often been judged by the number of volumes they lend. That measure is easy to count, but it overlooks services that do not involve borrowing a book. Libraries provide public workspaces, technology access, assistance with information searches, and programs that connect residents with community resources. These activities are difficult to compare with circulation totals because the benefits may appear later or outside the building.

The solution is not to stop counting borrowed materials. Circulation remains one useful indicator of demand. A more useful evaluation would pair it with other evidence, such as computer access, participation in public programs, and the needs of residents who have limited alternatives. Measuring more does not guarantee that every social benefit will be captured, but relying on circulation alone creates an unnecessarily narrow view of a public institution.

Question 1 : Central argument
Which statement best represents the author's position?

A. Libraries should discontinue circulation statistics because they are misleading.
B. Library performance should be assessed using circulation and additional measures of public service.
C. Technology access has become the primary purpose of public libraries.
D. All social benefits of libraries can be captured by a sufficiently large metric set.

Question 2 : Paragraph function
What is the main function of the second paragraph?

A. To reject the use of quantitative evaluation altogether.
B. To establish that circulation is the most reliable measure of a library's value.
C. To propose a broader approach while acknowledging its limits.
D. To compare the cost of borrowing books with the cost of technology access.

Question 3 : Supported inference
Which inference is most consistent with the passage?

A. A library with fewer loans may still provide substantial services to its community.
B. Libraries with the most public programs necessarily serve their communities best.
C. A library's value is unrelated to measurable outcomes.
