MCAT Practice Series

Commercial MCAT preparation can be prohibitively expensive for college students. When two SI Supervisors who performed well on the MCAT proposed a recurring practice series for UT students preparing for the exam, I developed their idea into a Spring 2027 pilot proposal for Sanger Learning Center leadership.

The MCAT Practice Series is a 14-week instructional program of 28 two-hour sessions that rotates through the four MCAT sections and follows the Supplemental Instruction session model. Sessions focus on passage analysis, scientific reasoning, and the application of problem-solving strategies. The series is not intended to replace comprehensive MCAT preparation courses.

The proposal introduces a fee-based enrollment structure: a $350 semester fee, an initial goal of 300 paying students, and 50–100 seats reserved at no cost for students demonstrating financial need. Additional sections open as enrollment grows, so staffing stays proportionate to demand.

Proposal abstract

Two SI Supervisors who performed well on the MCAT proposed a recurring practice series for UT students preparing for the exam. The roll out would include a 14-week sequence of collaborative question-analysis sessions that rotates through the four MCAT sections, built on the Supplemental Instruction session model.

The initial proposal outlined a no-cost, drop-in MCAT practice series facilitated within existing SI Supervisor appointments. The revised proposal maintains the original instructional framework and semester schedule while introducing a fee-based enrollment structure. Students would register for the semester at a cost of $350, with 50–100 seats reserved at no cost for students demonstrating financial need. Additional instructional sections would be introduced as enrollment increases, allowing staffing expenditures to remain proportionate to program demand.

The program's financial feasibility is supported by its use of existing instructional resources, established student staffing structures, and standardized lesson materials across sections. With a target enrollment of 300 paying students and 75 students receiving financial assistance, the program is projected to generate $105,000 in gross revenue and approximately $81,795 after anticipated refunds, direct operating expenses, and contingency. Under a more conservative enrollment scenario of 200 paying students, projected net revenue would be approximately $53,618. Each additional 50 paying students is expected to contribute approximately $13,000–$15,000 in additional net revenue, depending on staffing requirements.

Affordability and equitable access remain central considerations in the proposed enrollment structure. At $350 per semester, the series would provide a substantially less expensive option than commercial instructor-led MCAT preparation courses, while reserved no-cost enrollment would extend participation to students facing financial barriers. The program would provide structured, collaborative practice focused on passage analysis, scientific reasoning, and the application of problem-solving strategies across the four MCAT content areas.

Selected design and management decisions

Decision 1

Teach question analysis rather than deliver content lectures.

Rationale. The facilitators bring prior MCAT experience; the institutional advantage is guided reasoning practice. Commercial courses include content lectures, books, and full-length practice tests that this series does not provide.

Implementation. Each session begins with an independent attempt, proceeds through strategically formed pairs or small groups and a whole-class discussion, and ends with metacognitive error analysis and an exit ticket.

Decision 2

Protect existing supervisor time; pay added facilitators from fee revenue.

Rationale. The two originating supervisors already hold 10-hour appointments, and added sections need more facilitators than those appointments can cover.

Implementation. Each supervisor leads one two-hour session and spends one paid hour preparing inside their current appointment. Added lead and assistant facilitators are paid from fee revenue, and the coordinator workload map is a condition of launch.

Decision 3

Set sections from actual registrations.

Rationale. Revenue stays unknown until registration opens, and staffing cost should never run ahead of collected fees.

Implementation. On January 6, the enrollment gate sets one section for every 100 students, paid and no-fee combined. Facilitator offers made in December are contingent on the gate.

Decision 4

Lead with the no-fee option.

Rationale. Any mention of a fee can keep away students who would qualify for a no-fee seat.

Implementation. No-fee seats are confirmed through financial aid status, so Sanger never collects income documents. Promotion leads with the no-fee option, and students outside federal aid can submit a short request reviewed by the coordinator.

Learning model

03 / 10

The learning model is question-first. Students attempt a problem on their own before discussing their reasoning. Facilitators then guide the group through passage interpretation, evidence, calculations or experimental logic, answer-choice analysis, and transfer to a different question. Short explanations of underlying content are used only when a student is stuck on a problem-solving step.

Each session contains three to five substantial practice questions, with the exact number set by how complex the questions are. Careful analysis comes first; finishing a long question set matters much less.

Sessions follow the Supplemental Instruction model. After an icebreaker and a look at the lesson objectives, students attempt each question on their own, then work in strategically formed pairs or small groups to explain how they reached their answers, then bring that reasoning to a whole-class discussion of the correct answer and the wrong answers students most often choose.

Paid seats

Paid registration closes when sections are full or on January 6, 2027, whichever comes first. A full refund would be available until the student's second session; the model assumes 5% of fees are refunded.

No-fee seats

Between 50 and 100 seats carry no fee. They are intended for students with demonstrated financial need, confirmed through financial aid status so that Sanger never collects income documents. Students who have not filed for federal aid, including international students and students whose aid is still pending, could submit a short request reviewed by the coordinator. If requests exceed the reserved seats, a dated waitlist would fill seats released by paid withdrawals.

Each no-fee seat costs about $12 in materials, plus a share of facilitator time when it pushes enrollment past a 100-student section boundary. Within the modeled range, moving from 75 to 100 no-fee seats lowers net revenue by only about $330.

How the model works
  • Sections = students enrolled (paid plus no-fee) ÷ 100, rounded up. Each section meets twice a week.
  • Added lead hours per week = (session slots − 2) × 2.5. The two existing supervisors cover two slots inside their appointments.
  • Assistant hours per week = session slots × 2.
  • Facilitator pay = (added lead hours + assistant hours) × 14 weeks, plus 4 training hours for each added person, all at $17 per hour.
  • Net = gross fees − refunds (5%) − processing (3% of fees kept) − facilitator pay − materials ($12 per student + $500) − contingency (10% of direct costs).

Internal university outreach

Sanger Learning Center will coordinate with its existing marketing and outreach resources to develop promotional materials and distribute program information through established university channels. Primary outreach will include:

  • Health Professions Office: Collaborate with pre-health advisors to promote the series through advising appointments, newsletters, student communications, and pre-health events.
  • College of Natural Sciences: Coordinate with academic departments, faculty, and advisors to reach students enrolled in relevant science courses.
  • Sanger Learning Center: Promote the series through existing Supplemental Instruction sessions, student newsletters, campus events, social media, and outreach activities.
  • Pre-health student organizations: Partner with organizations such as Alpha Epsilon Delta and the American Medical Student Association to share registration information and offer brief presentations during organization meetings.
  • University communication platforms: Submit announcements to appropriate campus newsletters, university event calendars, and departmental communication channels.
Course-specific outreach

A significant portion of recruitment will focus on courses commonly taken by students preparing for medical school. With instructor approval, program information may be distributed through brief classroom announcements, course communication platforms, and printed materials.

Priority courses will include genetics, neuroscience, biochemistry, human physiology, anatomy, organic chemistry, physics, psychology, and sociology. Faculty teaching these courses will be invited to share a brief program announcement and registration link with students. Where appropriate, SI Leaders assigned to relevant courses may also provide information during their regular sessions.

Campus flyers and physical advertising

Printed flyers will be distributed throughout campus, with particular emphasis on science buildings, classrooms, academic advising areas, libraries, and high-traffic student spaces. Priority locations will include Welch Hall, the Norman Hackerman Building, Patterson Laboratories, and other buildings where pre-health students regularly attend classes.

Flyers will also be placed in approved locations outside the primary science facilities, including student gathering areas, residence halls, and campus-adjacent businesses that permit university program advertising. Promotional materials will prominently display the program dates, $350 semester fee, availability of no-cost seats, and a QR code linking directly to registration information. All physical advertising will follow university posting requirements and applicable building-specific approval procedures.

External digital marketing and student communities

External outreach will focus on online platforms frequently used by UT Austin students and prospective medical school applicants.

  • Reddit: Explore opportunities to promote the series through relevant communities, particularly r/UTAustin and other pre-medical discussion forums, where community rules permit institutional program announcements. Posts will explain the program's purpose, instructional approach, cost, and eligibility.
  • Instagram: Develop short promotional posts and graphics for distribution through Sanger accounts, student organizations, and university-affiliated student networks.
  • Student organization social media: Provide pre-health organizations with ready-to-share graphics and registration information for their established audiences.
  • Student referrals: Encourage enrolled students, SI Leaders, and student organization representatives to share information about the series with peers preparing for the MCAT.
  • Campus-adjacent community spaces: Explore opportunities to display flyers in nearby coffee shops, study spaces, and student housing communities, subject to permission.

Paid digital advertising and opportunities through Texas Student Media may be considered if initial registration is below projections and funding is available within the approved marketing budget.

Rollout pain points and risks

07 / 10

Charging a fee is the hardest part of this program to launch. These are the problems it has to solve first, with the response planned for each.

Approval to charge a fee

A university unit usually needs sign-off before it charges students. A new fee takes time to approve and may not be ready by Spring 2027.

Response Submit in November; December 15 decision point.

Confirming aid eligibility

Tying no-fee seats to financial aid means coordinating with the financial aid office and getting approval to confirm a student's status, without Sanger seeing any financial records. Self-reporting is simpler but harder to check.

Response Request a yes/no status check from Financial Aid.

Students outside federal aid

Some students who cannot afford the fee may not qualify for aid, including international students and students whose aid status is still pending.

Response Short request form reviewed by the coordinator.

A semester fee in a drop-in program

The series was first designed as drop-in, with an RSVP for each session. A semester fee assumes semester enrollment, so the registration path changes and refund rules must cover students who stop coming.

Response Semester registration; refund window closes after the second session.

Collecting payment

Payments and refunds need a university-approved payment system and an account to receive the funds.

Response Request the account with the fee approval.

Deterring the students it is meant for

Any mention of a fee can keep away students who would qualify for a no-fee seat.

Response Promotion leads with the no-fee option and makes it easy to claim.

Coordinator workload

Administering fees, no-fee seats, payments, and refunds adds work to a role that is already a condition of the staffing plan.

Response Workload map; fewer sections if it does not fit.

Unknown revenue

Revenue depends on how many students pay, which stays unknown until registration opens.

Response The January 6 enrollment gate ties staffing to actual fees.

If the fee is not approved by mid-December, I recommend one of two fallbacks: run the October no-fee pilot in one section and test the fee system in a later term, or move the fee-based launch to Summer 2027, when many students take the MCAT and there is time to market the series properly.

Conditions to confirm before launch

  • Fee approval. The university approves a $350 program fee and a revenue account that credits Sanger.
  • Payment method. Students can pay through an approved university system, with refunds handled the same way.
  • Refund and withdrawal policy. Written and published with the registration page.
  • No-fee seat process. Eligibility confirmed through financial aid status, plus a review path for students without aid on file.
  • Facilitators. Enough lead and assistant facilitators recruited and trained for the number of sections enrolled.
  • Rooms. Spaces of 100 or 200 seats with group-work layouts reserved for each section.
  • Materials. All passages and questions original or properly licensed.
  • Workload map. Coordinator and supervisor duties documented, with no unpaid hours.

A1

Project Scope and Charter

What the pilot will and will not deliver, under which constraints, and what must be true before it launches.

Project objectives

Revenue

Generate net revenue toward Sanger in a single semester, with a paid goal of 300 students at a $350 semester fee.

Access

Reserve 50–100 no-fee seats for students with demonstrated financial need, confirmed through financial aid status.

Instructional value

Deliver practice in passage analysis, scientific reasoning, quantitative setup, and error diagnosis across all four MCAT sections.

Workload

Keep supervisor MCAT work within three paid hours a week and run the pilot with no unpaid hours.

Evidence

Record enrollment, attendance, usefulness, and cost per student for the decision to repeat the series.

Scope boundaries

In scope
  • A 14-week semester calendar
  • Two sessions per week in every section
  • Shared lesson materials across sections
  • Semester registration, payment, and refunds
  • No-fee seat process
  • Recruitment and outreach
  • Session feedback and the May pilot report
Out of scope
  • Individualized tutoring
  • Comprehensive content lectures
  • Books, question banks, and full-length practice tests
  • Official score prediction or guaranteed score increases
  • Unlicensed commercial or AAMC materials

Staffing & budget constraints

  • Two supervisors × 3 MCAT hours per week × 14 weeks = 84 existing paid hours ($1,428 at the planning rate), not counted as new cost
  • Added lead and assistant facilitators paid from fee revenue at $17 per hour
  • One section per 100 enrolled students; an assistant facilitator in every session
  • Coordinator time is tracked; the pilot never depends on unpaid hours
  • Copyrighted MCAT preparation licenses are not assumed

Conditions to confirm before launch

  • Fee approval and a revenue account that credits Sanger.
  • Payment through an approved university system, with refunds handled the same way.
  • Refund and withdrawal policy published with the registration page.
  • No-fee seat process confirmed through financial aid status, with a review path.
  • Lead and assistant facilitators recruited and trained for the sections enrolled.
  • Rooms of 100 or 200 seats with group-work layouts.
  • All passages and questions original or properly licensed.
  • Workload map with no unpaid hours.

Five-question session feedback form

  1. The session helped me identify the reasoning needed to approach an MCAT-style question. (1–5)
  2. I can explain why a plausible incorrect answer was not supported. (1–5)
  3. The pace allowed enough time to work independently and discuss reasoning. (1–5)

Original MCAT-style instructional examples, not official AAMC practice questions. Each lesson follows the Supplemental Instruction session model: students work on a question independently first, then explain how they got their answers in pairs or small groups, then the whole room discusses the reasoning and the tempting wrong answers.
Facilitation techniques used in these lessons
Think, write, pair, shareStudents work silently, write an answer and a one-line reason, explain it to a partner, then share with the room.
Strategic pairingThe facilitator forms pairs on purpose: students with different answers, or a confident student with a less confident one.
Fist to fiveStudents show 0 to 5 fingers for confidence. Used to check objectives and to form mixed pairs.
Vote and revoteEveryone commits to a letter, discusses, then votes again. Changed votes start the whole-class talk.
Numbered heads togetherGroups of 3 or 4 number off and agree on an answer. The facilitator calls a number, and that student explains for the group.
Wait timeFive to ten seconds of silence after a question before anyone answers.
RedirectingThe facilitator answers a question with a question, or sends it back to the group: "What does your partner think?"
Answer choice autopsyGroups explain why each wrong choice is wrong, using a line of evidence.
JigsawEach group takes one piece (a choice, a paragraph, a design) and teaches it to the room.
Gallery walkGroups post their work, rotate, and leave a question or comment on another group's sheet.

0–5 · Welcome and icebreaker

Headline it. Each student writes a six-word headline for their week and reads it to a neighbor, who names the main point in their own words. Debrief: a headline states a claim; the details are support. That is what we will look for in a passage today.

5–10 · Lesson objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  1. Name the author's main claim in one sentence before reading the answer choices.
  2. Label what each paragraph does in the argument.
  3. Reject answer choices that claim more than the passage supports.

Facilitator Read the objectives aloud. Students show fist to five on "I can find an author's main point." Note the 0–2s; they will be paired with 4–5s.

Student handout · passage and questions

Original practice passage:

Public libraries have often been judged by the number of volumes they lend. That measure is easy to count, but it overlooks services that do not involve borrowing a book. Libraries provide public workspaces, technology access, assistance with information searches, and programs that connect residents with community resources. These activities are difficult to compare with circulation totals because the benefits may appear later or outside the building.

The solution is not to stop counting borrowed materials. Circulation remains one useful indicator of demand. A more useful evaluation would pair it with other evidence, such as computer access, participation in public programs, and the needs of residents who have limited alternatives. Measuring more does not guarantee that every social benefit will be captured, but relying on circulation alone creates an unnecessarily narrow view of a public institution.

Question 1 : Central argument
Which statement best represents the author's position?

A. Libraries should discontinue circulation statistics because they are misleading.
B. Library performance should be assessed using circulation and additional measures of public service.
C. Technology access has become the primary purpose of public libraries.
D. All social benefits of libraries can be captured by a sufficiently large metric set.

Question 2 : Paragraph function
What is the main function of the second paragraph?

A. To reject the use of quantitative evaluation altogether.
B. To establish that circulation is the most reliable measure of a library's value.
C. To propose a broader approach while acknowledging its limits.
D. To compare the cost of borrowing books with the cost of technology access.

Question 3 : Supported inference
Which inference is most consistent with the passage?

A. A library with fewer loans may still provide substantial services to its community.
B. Libraries with the most public programs necessarily serve their communities best.
C. A library's value is unrelated to measurable outcomes.
D. Computer access is the strongest predictor of library effectiveness.

Question 1 · individual, then pairs or groups, then whole class

1 · Individual10–30

Think, write

Read the passage silently. Before looking at choices, write the author's position in one sentence. Answer Questions 1 and 2 and circle your confidence.

Facilitator Circulate and observe without confirming answers. Note which letters students choose for Q1.

2 · Pairs or groups30–55

Strategic pairing by answer: "Hold up your letter for Question 1." Pair a student who chose B with a student who chose A or D.

Paragraph Job Cards: each pair labels the job of each paragraph and underlines the clause that limits the author's claim. Each partner explains how they got their answer to Q1, pointing to a line.

Facilitator Listen for students who quote the first paragraph only. Ask: "Where does the author say what to keep?"

3 · Whole class55–75

Vote and revote on Q1, then an Answer Choice Autopsy: each pair convicts one wrong choice with a line from the passage.

Facilitator Use wait time before calling on anyone. Redirect questions back to the room: "Who sees it differently?"

Facilitator promptsWhich clause limits the author's claim?Does the answer choice make a claim stronger than the passage?Is this evidence of the conclusion or merely an example?
Question 1Correct: B

The passage retains circulation as useful evidence but argues against treating it as sufficient. A is too absolute, C assigns priority the author does not establish, and D contradicts the passage's qualification.

D"More measures" sounds like the author's proposal, so "all benefits captured" feels close enough.Circle the absolute words ("all") and match them to "does not guarantee."

Partners compare answers and the word in each choice that decided it for them.

The paragraph proposes a combined assessment and notes that no set of measurements captures every effect.

100–115 · Error analysis

Students review incorrect responses and identify the underlying reasoning errors, including overly broad interpretations, unsupported inferences, or misidentification of a paragraph's purpose within the author's argument. Working in pairs, students discuss the passage evidence that distinguishes the correct response from plausible alternatives and identify strategies for improving their approach.

115–120 · Exit ticket

Identify one answer you selected and explain which evidence from the passage supports your reasoning. How did that evidence help you distinguish the correct answer from the alternatives?

0–5 · Welcome and icebreaker

Cause or Correlation? The facilitator presents two everyday observations: "Every time I wash my car, it rains" and "When I drink coffee late in the evening, I sleep less." Working in pairs, students discuss whether each observation provides sufficient evidence to establish a causal relationship. Students identify possible alternative explanations and consider what additional information or experimental conditions would be necessary to evaluate each claim.

Debrief: The facilitator guides a brief discussion distinguishing correlation from causation, emphasizing the importance of controlled comparisons, confounding variables, and evidence-based conclusions when interpreting experimental results.

5–10 · Lesson objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  1. Analyze experimental design by identifying independent and dependent variables, controlled conditions, and their respective roles in an investigation.
  2. Interpret experimental data by identifying observed relationships, distinguishing evidence from inference, and recognizing the limitations of conclusions drawn from the results.
  3. Evaluate experimental approaches by selecting an appropriate follow-up investigation to test a proposed biological mechanism and explaining how the results could support or challenge the hypothesis.

Facilitator Students show fist to five on "I can read an experiment table." Record the spread; it sets the groups.

Student handout · passage and questions

Original experimental scenario:

A laboratory examines an enzyme that converts substrate S into product P. Researchers measure the initial reaction rate at a fixed substrate concentration with increasing concentrations of compound X. Three trials are collected per condition. The values below are fictional means used solely for practice.

Compound X (µM) Mean initial rate (µmol/min)
0 24
2 18
4 12
8 6

Question 1 : What can be concluded?
Which conclusion is best supported by the table?

A. Compound X is associated with lower measured initial reaction rates under these conditions.
B. Compound X causes the enzyme to lose its primary structure.
C. Compound X lowers substrate concentration in every tested condition.
D. Compound X increases the enzyme's maximum reaction rate.

Question 2 : Experimental variable
Which variable was manipulated deliberately?

A. Mean initial reaction rate.
B. Product P concentration after completion.
C. Concentration of compound X.
D. Number of trials per condition.

Question 3 : Follow-up design
Which next experiment would best help distinguish whether X competes with substrate S for the enzyme's binding site?

A. Repeat the experiment with only a larger volume of reaction mixture.
B. Measure initial rate across a range of substrate concentrations, with and without X.
C. Measure the rate once more at 8 µM X, using the same substrate concentration.
D. Increase the number of trials only at 0 µM X.

Question 1 · individual, then pairs or groups, then whole class

1 · Individual10–30

Think, write

Study the table. Write three lines: what changed, what was measured, what stayed the same. Then answer Questions 1 and 2.

Facilitator Watch for students who answer from memory about inhibitors instead of the table.

2 · Pairs or groups30–55

Groups of three mixed by fist-to-five rating: one high, one middle, one low.

Experiment Puzzle: groups sort variable and control cards into manipulated, measured, and held constant. Each member explains how they got Q1, starting with the row of the table they used.

Facilitator If a group cites denaturing or binding sites, ask: "Which number in the table shows that?"

3 · Whole class55–75

Evidence Match: the class ranks choices A–D for Q1 from strongest to weakest support on the board, then votes.

Facilitator Call on groups whose ranking differed. Use wait time and redirect claims back to the data.

Facilitator promptsWhich statements are directly observed?What is held constant?Could a different mechanism produce this result?
Question 1Correct: A

The table supports a dose-associated reduction at one fixed substrate concentration. It does not establish the inhibition mechanism, substrate depletion, or Vmax.

Tempting choiceWhy students choose itFacilitator move
BStudents recall that some inhibitors denature enzymes and bring that knowledge in.Ask which column of the table measures structure. None does.
CA falling rate looks like the substrate is running out.Point out that substrate concentration was fixed by design.

1 · Individual75–82

Think, write

Answer Question 3 independently in five minutes.

2 · Pairs or groups82–90

Jigsaw: each group is assigned one design option (A–D).

Each group argues for or against its design: what would it test, and what would it leave unanswered?

3 · Whole class90–100

Groups present in order. The class decides which design separates competitive inhibition from other explanations, then applies the same method to a new graph from the facilitator.

X concentration changes by design; rate is the dependent measurement, and the number of trials is fixed.

Student handout · passage and questions

Original scenario:

A fluid sample with a mass of 0.30 kg is warmed. Assume its specific heat capacity is 4.0 × 10³ J/(kg·°C), and ignore heat loss to the environment. The temperature increases by 5.0°C.

Question 1 : Energy calculation
How much energy is transferred to the sample?

A. 60 J
B. 600 J
C. 6,000 J
D. 60,000 J

Question 2 : Changed condition
If the mass doubles while the heat capacity and temperature change remain the same, the required energy will:

A. Decrease by half.
B. Remain unchanged.
C. Double.
D. Increase by a factor of four.

Question 3 : Dimensional analysis
Which combination of units yields energy in joules using Q = mcΔT?

A. kg × J/(kg·°C) × °C
B. kg × J/°C × °C
C. kg × J/kg × °C
D. kg × J/(kg·°C) × 1/°C

Question 1 · individual, then pairs or groups, then whole class

1 · Individual10–30

Think, write

Estimate First: write a predicted order of magnitude for Question 1. Then calculate with every unit written out.

Facilitator Look for work with no units, and for estimates that differ from the final answer by a factor of ten or more.

2 · Pairs or groups30–55

Line up and fold: students line up by their estimate, then the line folds so the highest pairs with the lowest.

Equation Match: pairs match cards to knowns, unknowns, and the governing equation. Each partner walks through how they got their answer while the other checks units line by line.

Facilitator When a pair disagrees, ask them to find the step where their numbers split.

3 · Whole class55–75

The facilitator writes three wrong setups on the board. The class identifies the unit check or estimate that would have caught each one.

Facilitator Use wait time. Ask the room before confirming: "Which of these would you trust, and why?"

Facilitator promptsWhat unit must the answer have?What changes proportionally when mass doubles?Does the calculated magnitude match the estimate?
Question 1Correct: C

Q = mcΔT = (0.30 kg)(4.0 × 10³ J/kg·°C)(5.0°C) = 6.0 × 10³ J. All remaining units cancel to joules.

Tempting choiceWhy students choose itFacilitator move
BStudents multiply 0.30 by 4.0 and lose a power of ten in the scientific notation.Ask them to compare the answer with their estimate.
DStudents read 0.30 kg as 3.0 kg.Have them restate each known with its unit before calculating.

2 · Pairs or groups82–90

Partners swap papers.

Peer unit check: each partner verifies the other's units and proportional reasoning and writes one note.

3 · Whole class90–100

Fist to five on confidence, then the facilitator gives one variation (halve the temperature change) for the whole room to solve aloud.

Q is directly proportional to mass if c and ΔT are fixed.

DStudents carry over a squared relationship from kinetic energy.Ask: where is the exponent in Q = mcΔT?

Student handout · passage and questions

Original scenario:

A student research team surveys 240 undergraduates at the end of a semester. Students report their average weekly study-group attendance and perceived academic confidence. Students who attended study groups more often also report greater confidence. The research team did not assign students to attend groups, and the survey did not measure their prior confidence or course difficulty.

Question 1 : Supported conclusion
Which statement is most consistent with this design?

A. Study-group attendance caused increases in academic confidence.
B. Greater confidence necessarily causes students to attend study groups.
C. Attendance and reported confidence were positively associated in the surveyed students.
D. Study groups are ineffective because participants selected their own attendance.

Question 2 : Confounding
Which omitted factor could plausibly affect both study-group attendance and confidence?

A. A student's preexisting academic motivation.
B. The exact date of the survey only, if identical for all students.
C. A fixed survey instruction read to every participant.
D. The number of response options shown on a single question, if identical for all students.

Question 3 : Stronger design
Which approach would most improve a future study's ability to assess a causal effect of access to study-group sessions?

A. Survey only those who already attend frequently.
B. Randomly assign eligible volunteers to an offered-session group or an appropriate comparison condition, while following ethical and operational safeguards.
C. Remove students who report low confidence from the analysis.
D. Ask all participants whether they believe study groups are helpful.

Question 1 · individual, then pairs or groups, then whole class

1 · Individual10–30

Think, write

Draw the study: write the two measured variables and what was not measured. Answer Question 1.

Facilitator Note students who draw an arrow from attendance to confidence.

2 · Pairs or groups30–55

Numbered heads together: count off into groups of four so friends are split up.

Fix the Experiment: groups build a three-variable causal sketch with variable and confound cards. Each member explains how they got Q1.

Facilitator If a group settles on cause, ask: "Could the arrow run the other way?"

3 · Whole class55–75

Concept Pair Discrimination: the class fills an association versus causation chart. The facilitator calls a number, and that student defends the group's strongest alternative explanation.

Facilitator Redirect challenges between groups rather than settling them from the front.

Facilitator promptsWhat was actually measured?Could the direction run the other way?Which groups would differ before any intervention?
Question 1Correct: C

This observational survey establishes an association in the sample, not the direction or existence of causation.

Tempting choiceWhy students choose itFacilitator move
AIt matches what students believe about study groups.Ask what the researchers controlled. They assigned nothing.
DStudents overcorrect: if the study is weak, the program must not work.Ask whether a weak design can prove ineffectiveness either.
