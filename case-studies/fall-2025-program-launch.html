The problem

Each fall the Sanger Learning Center launches a new cohort of SI Leaders, peer educators who run weekly study sessions for 2,500+ students in historically difficult courses across 13 undergraduate colleges. By spring 2025, three things needed to be true at once:

  • The training curriculum had to be redesigned.
  • A layer of administrative and compliance content had to be added: Canvas setup, hours logging, safety, and mandated-reporter training.
  • The operational rollout had to run on time: room reservations, session times, leader assignments, Canvas pages, and faculty introductions.

These were linked. Curriculum decisions set the training schedule, the schedule set when rooms were needed, and leader-to-room assignments depended on cohort-time data from the registrar that does not exist until late summer. A leader who walks into week one without a room, a Canvas page, or a faculty contact has been let down by the program, however good the training was.

Discovery

In early May I interviewed current SI Leaders and the supervisor team and synthesized their pain points. Three findings shaped the design:

  1. One training format for everyone was failing two groups. New leaders needed slower, foundational work; returning leaders needed depth, a refresh, and time with the community.
  2. Much of the foundational content was delivered live but did not need to be. Leaders said they would rather work through it on their own time and keep live sessions for what benefits from being in a room together.
  3. Returning leaders consistently named the community and connection time as the most valuable part of training.

The administrative and compliance topics were scoped at the same time with the supervisor team, since none of them existed in the previous curriculum.

Design decisions

  1. Split training into three tracks by audience

    Design challenge
    A single format was too repetitive for returning leaders and too fast for new ones.
    Decision
    Run three sessions on separate days, each with its own facilitator guide: New Leader Training (in person), an All-Leader Virtual session, and an All-Leader In-Person day.
    Rationale
    Segmenting by experience lets each group get the pace and depth it needs, while the shared sessions keep one common standard across the cohort.
    Implementation
    New leaders got a half-day of SI foundations, facilitation techniques, scenario practice, and role play. The in-person all-leader day was built around community, advanced facilitation workshops, and supervisor one-on-one time.

  2. Move foundational content into a self-paced module

    Design challenge
    Live training time was being spent on material leaders could learn alone, which crowded out practice and connection.
    Decision
    Build the foundational curriculum as an interactive self-paced module completed before the in-person session.
    Rationale
    Live time is scarce and is best used for what only works live: scenario practice, community, supervisor contact, and nuanced conversation.
    Implementation
    Leaders arrived at in-person training having already drafted a first lesson plan, a professor email, and a class pitch inside the module, so the session could work from those drafts.

  3. Deliver the compliance layer as a recorded virtual session

    Design challenge
    Five new administrative and compliance topics applied to every leader, new and returning, and some carried attestation requirements.
    Decision
    Cover them in one synchronous Zoom session for all leaders, recorded for later access, with a compliance attestation.
    Rationale
    The content is procedural and identical for everyone, so it did not need in-person time, and a recording supports leaders who need to look up a process later.
    Implementation
    Canvas operations, logging work hours, safety and compliance (including FERPA basics and escalation), mandated reporting, and administrative operations.

  4. Plan operations backward from the data dependencies

    Design challenge
    Session times depend on student schedule data that arrives late in the summer, but rooms have to be requested much earlier.
    Decision
    Reserve a room block with the registrar in June and July, then map session times and assign leaders in July and August as data arrived.
    Rationale
    Securing capacity early kept the late-summer work to matching, which could be done quickly once the data was in hand.
    Implementation
    Two session slots per leader, with college building preferences and walking distance from the supported course taken into account.

The onboarding module

The self-paced module is about four hours of training, designed to be completed over several days in four sittings of about an hour. It is a single-page web app with 21 screens in six sections: SI foundations, a facilitation toolkit, preparation for the first session, a professor email generator, lesson planning, and a closing summary. Its builders produce working drafts: a five-step lesson plan, a professor email with a live preview, a 60-second class pitch, and a weekly schedule. It also includes 12 branching scenario moments, an 83-strategy library, and knowledge checks throughout.

Progress saves in the learner's browser, so each sitting picks up where the last one ended. The sidebar marks where each sitting starts, and a stopping-point card closes each of the first three.

The program launched on schedule in late August 2025: leaders started the semester with rooms, session times, Canvas pages, and faculty contacts in place.

These are delivery outcomes. This case study does not report learner-level data such as training satisfaction, session observation results, or student attendance, so it makes no claim about how the redesign changed session quality.

Lessons

Treat it as one project. Curriculum and operations were planned together because each depended on the other. Sequencing around the late-arriving registrar data mattered more than any single workstream.

Spend live time on what needs a room. Moving foundations into the module freed the in-person day for practice and community, the part returning leaders valued most.

What I would add next cycle. ProposedA short post-training survey for each track, a comparison of first-month peer-observation results against the prior year, and completion data from the module, so the next redesign can rest on learner evidence as well as interviews.
