Foundations/Screen 00/Course overview

Sharing data, protecting people.

Organizations share data with vendors, researchers, and partners every day. Removing names rarely eliminates privacy risk. This module explains how released data can still identify individuals and provides a structured method for reviewing a proposed release.

After completing this module, you will be able to review a proposed data release and recommend, with a documented rationale, whether it should proceed with safeguards, be modified, be escalated, or be declined.

Learning objectives. You will be able to:
  1. Classify attributes as direct identifiers, quasi-identifiers, or sensitive attributes, and explain when a classification depends on context, such as how many people share a job title.Practice 01Check 12, 13
  2. Trace how personal data moves through a real-time bidding auction and identify where disclosure occurs.Practice 02Check 13
  3. Explain how external data enabled re-identification in documented cases, and distinguish record uniqueness from the identification of an individual.Practice 03, 05, 06Check 12, 13
  4. Distinguish pseudonymization, which replaces identifiers with codes that can be traced back to individuals, from anonymization, and select techniques for modifying data before release.Practice 07, 08Check 12, 13
  5. Evaluate whether records in a dataset can be isolated, linked to other data, or used to infer sensitive information, using the k-anonymity and l-diversity measures where they apply.Practice 09, 10, 11Check 12, 13
  6. Recommend and justify a release decision for a realistic proposal.Practice 04Check 12, 13

Foundations/Screen 01/Sort activity

Identifying personal data and re-identification risk

Under GDPR Article 4(1), personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. U.S. state privacy laws use similar definitions. The term identifiable is central to this module: a record can relate to an individual after the name has been removed.

Practitioners classify attributes on two separate dimensions: whether an attribute identifies an individual on its own or only in combination with other data, and how much harm its disclosure could cause. The three cards below cover both dimensions.

Identification type 1

Direct identifiers

Attributes that identify an individual without additional information.

Full namePersonal emailPhone numberGovernment ID numberAccount number

Organizations usually remove direct identifiers first when preparing a release. Removing them alone rarely prevents re-identification.

Identification type 2

Quasi-identifiers

Attributes shared by many people that can identify an individual when combined with each other or with external data.

ZIP codeDate of birthSexJob titleEvent datesLocation history

They often remain in a dataset after names are removed, so most of this module addresses how to manage them.

A separate category: potential harm

Sensitive attributes

Attributes whose disclosure could cause disproportionate harm. A sensitive attribute may or may not identify anyone.

Health conditionPrecise geolocationFinancial account detailsBiometric data

A release can expose sensitive values without identifying anyone, so reviewers assess sensitivity and identifiability separately.

These frameworks overlap but are not interchangeable. A date of birth is a quasi-identifier and is not usually treated as sensitive. A diagnosis is sensitive and falls under Article 9, yet on its own it rarely identifies anyone.

87%

Limitations of removing names

Using 1990 U.S. Census data, Sweeney (2000) estimated that about 87% of the U.S. population was likely to be unique on three attributes: 5-digit ZIP code, full date of birth, and sex. Golle (2006) repeated the analysis with 2000 Census data and estimated about 63%.

Uniqueness does not establish identity. Identifying an individual also requires a second dataset that contains the same attributes alongside names, such as a voter registration list. Screen 05 describes this linkage in a documented case.

The HIPAA Safe Harbor method (45 CFR §164.514(b)(2)) reflects this risk. Among its 18 identifier types, it requires removing all elements of dates except the year, and it limits ZIP codes to their first three digits, or removes them entirely in sparsely populated areas.

How personal data moves through a real-time bidding auction

Much of the advertising on websites and in apps is sold through real-time bidding (RTB), an automated auction that runs while a page loads. The IAB Tech Lab's OpenRTB specification defines the message format. Each exchange sets its own response deadline, measured in milliseconds.

DSPA demand-side platform (DSP) evaluates bid requests and places bids on behalf of advertisers.

Variation between auctions

Where releases fail/Screen 03/Case study · Map exploration

Case study: how aggregated activity data exposed sensitive locations

In November 2017, Strava released an updated Global Heatmap built from more than one billion activities uploaded by its users. On 27 January 2018, Nathan Ruser, an Australian university student, posted that the map revealed activity patterns at military sites. News reports over the following days described visible patterns at bases in several countries.

The heatmap displayed aggregated, historical activity. It did not show names or live positions. Release reviewers therefore need to assess what aggregated data can still reveal.

Where releases fail/Screen 04/Sort activity · Decision

Evaluating a proposed release: purpose and safeguards

A legitimate purpose is necessary for a release, and it is not sufficient. Reviewers also ask whether the data is limited to what the purpose needs and whether the recipient is bound by adequate controls.

Two GDPR principles frame the review. Purpose limitation means collecting and using data only for specified, explicit purposes. Data minimisation means limiting data to what is necessary for those purposes (Article 5(1)(b) and (c)). U.S. state privacy laws and most internal data-governance policies set comparable expectations.

Where releases fail/Screen 05/Two investigative case walkthroughs

How external datasets can enable re-identification

Two documented cases show how external information, a purchased voter list in one case and public social-media connections in the other, linked records or accounts described as anonymous to identified individuals.

InstructionsSelect a case, then select Reveal next step to progress through it and answer the question before the final step. A comparison of the two cases appears when both are complete.
  1. 01

    In the 1990s, the Massachusetts Group Insurance Commission (GIC), which bought health insurance for state employees, released records of employees' hospital visits to researchers. Names, addresses, and Social Security numbers had been removed. Governor William Weld said publicly that patient privacy was protected.

  2. 02

    The records kept ZIP code, full date of birth, and sex. Latanya Sweeney, then a graduate student at MIT, paid $20 for the Cambridge voter rolls, which listed the same three attributes alongside names and addresses.

  3. 03

    In the voter list, six people shared Weld's date of birth, three of them were men, and only one lived in his ZIP code. His hospitalization in May 1996 had been widely reported. Sweeney matched those attributes to his hospital records and sent them to his office.

  4. 04

    Sweeney's later analysis of 1990 Census data estimated that about 87% of the U.S. population was likely unique on ZIP code, date of birth, and sex. Later reviews note this case's limits: the voter list covered only part of Cambridge's population, and the publicized hospitalization narrowed the search (Barth-Jones, 2012).

Step 1 of 4

Case findings

Removing names did not make the records anonymous. The release kept attributes that also appeared, with names, in a public list, and public knowledge of the hospitalization narrowed the search further. A release review should identify which external sources share the released attributes.

  1. 01

    In October 2019, Senator Mitt Romney told The Atlantic's McKay Coppins that he used a Twitter account under another name to follow political discussion. He declined to give its name but mentioned that it followed 668 accounts.

  2. 02

    That remark confirmed two facts anyone could check: the account existed, and its following count was about 668.

  3. 03

    Slate writer Ashley Feinberg reviewed accounts following members of Romney's family and found a low-activity account following one of his grandchildren. Its likes, replies, and follows fit what a Romney account would be expected to show.

  4. 04

    The account, named Pierre Delecto, matched. Feinberg published her findings in Slate, and Romney then confirmed to Coppins that the account was his.

Step 1 of 4

Case findings

No dataset was published here. Feinberg linked a live pseudonymous account to its owner by combining a detail Romney disclosed with public social connections. Attributes that appeared harmless individually became identifying once they could be matched against an external source.

Controls/Screen 07/Tabbed comparison

Three approaches to de-identifying data before release

Organizations describe data as "anonymized" even when it has been handled in very different ways. The three approaches below reflect common practice, ordered from least to most protective. Privacy laws and standards do not define them as formal categories.

GDPR defines pseudonymisation in Article 4(5). Under Recital 26, data falls outside the regulation only when individuals can no longer be identified by any means reasonably likely to be used, such as matching the data against other datasets. NIST SP 800-188 and ISO/IEC 20889 describe a wider range of techniques. Whether a particular release can be considered anonymous depends on the method used, who receives the data, and what other information that recipient can access.

InstructionsSelect each of the three tabs to compare the approaches.

Replace identifiers with codes and protect the link to identities

Names and other direct identifiers are replaced with codes. The information that connects each code to an individual's identity, often called the re-identification key, is stored separately and protected against unauthorized access, as GDPR Article 4(5) requires.

Pseudonymization reduces specific risks. Staff who work with the data do not see names, and a breach of the working dataset exposes less. Because each person keeps the same code, analysts can still follow an individual's records over time, which longitudinal research and fraud analysis require.

Usefulness for analysisHigh. Each individual's records keep their full detail.
Effort requiredLow. The organization must protect the key and control who can use it.

Limitations

Pseudonymized data is still personal data for anyone who can re-identify it by reasonable means, including the organization that holds the key (GDPR Recital 26; EDPB Guidelines 01/2025). The details left unchanged can still be matched to external data, and anyone who obtains the key can re-identify every record.

Remove or modify identifying details permanently

Direct identifiers are removed. Quasi-identifiers, such as ZIP code and date of birth, are removed, made less precise, or altered (Screen 08). No record is kept that would allow the changes to be reversed. Many organizations mean this approach when they call a dataset anonymized.

Applied carefully, it substantially lowers the likelihood of identifying individuals. Applied loosely, it leaves enough detail for records to be matched to other data: the Massachusetts release on Screen 05 removed names but kept full ZIP code, date of birth, and sex.

This approach applies the changes from approach B and adds a documented assessment of whether the recipient, or anyone who obtains the data, could identify individuals using other data they can reasonably access. The assessment checks whether records can be isolated, linked, or used to infer sensitive information (Screen 09), and may use the k-anonymity and l-diversity measures (Screens 10 and 11).

This approach aligns most closely with regulatory expectations for data described as anonymous. It also requires the most from an organization: specialist expertise, time, and periodic review.

Transformation techniques for reducing identifiability

ISO/IEC 20889 groups de-identification techniques into families, including suppression, pseudonymization, generalization, randomization, and synthetic data, alongside statistical and cryptographic tools. The five described below are those release reviewers encounter most often. Each reduces specific risks at some cost to analytical utility.

InstructionsOpen each technique to review its purpose, an example, its principal limitation, and appropriate uses. Then answer the selection question that follows.

Purpose

Remove attributes or individual values that identify an individual or are too rare to release.

Example
name: Jordan Ellis · employee_id: E-20417 · age: 97
name: [removed] · employee_id: [removed] · age: [suppressed]
Principal limitation

Quasi-identifiers that remain in the data can still be matched to external data. Removing many values makes the data less useful and can distort analytical results.

When it may be appropriate

Always for direct identifiers the purpose does not need, and for rare values, such as an age over 89, that single out individuals.

Purpose

Replace identifiers with codes that stay the same for each person, so that an individual's records can be connected without revealing who the individual is.

Example
patient: Jordan Ellis · visits: 2026-01-04, 2026-02-11
patient: P-7Q42 · visits: 2026-01-04, 2026-02-11
Principal limitation

The data remains personal data for anyone who can reverse the codes or link them to other records. Because each person always receives the same code, separate releases can be joined.

When it may be appropriate

Longitudinal analysis where the recipient must follow the same person, with the table linking codes to names held separately by the data owner.

Purpose

Reduce precision so that more records share each value.

Example
age: 47 · ZIP: 78704 · admitted: 2026-03-12
age: 45–49 · ZIP: 787** · admitted: 2026-03
Principal limitation

Coarse values lose analytical detail. Generalization alone does not prevent inference when a group shares one sensitive value (Screen 11).

When it may be appropriate

Making records less distinctive, so that each combination of identifying details is shared by enough people to meet a k-anonymity threshold (Screen 10).

Purpose

Alter values so that individual records are less reliable while totals and averages stay close to the original.

Noise addition
salary: 98,400 · 45,100 · 61,750
salary: 97,900 · 46,300 · 61,200
Permutation
Salaries swapped among records within the same department and grade, so the department total is unchanged.
Principal limitation

If the changes are too small, analysts can sometimes estimate the original values; if they are too large, the data becomes less useful. A record that is unique for other reasons, such as the only executive in a region, can remain recognizable.

When it may be appropriate

Numeric attributes used for statistical analysis, where exact individual values are not needed.

Purpose

Generate artificial records that reproduce the structure and statistical patterns of the original data.

Example
2,400 real support tickets with customer details
2,400 generated tickets with the same fields, formats, and category mix
Principal limitation

Models can memorize and reproduce real outliers, and the results of complex analyses may differ from those on the original data. Privacy needs to be tested rather than assumed.

When it may be appropriate

Software testing, development, training, and exploratory analysis.

Controls/Screen 09/Expandable cards · Classification check

WP29 re-identification risk categories

In Opinion 05/2014 on Anonymisation Techniques (WP216), the Article 29 Working Party (WP29), the former advisory body of EU data protection authorities, evaluates anonymization techniques against three risks: singling out, linkability, and inference. The risks are related, and one dataset can present more than one, but each addresses a different question.

In July 2026, the European Data Protection Board released draft Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation for public consultation. The draft restates the same ideas as three criteria: no record isolation, no linkage, and no inference.

InstructionsOpen each risk category to review its definition, example, detection method, and mitigation. Then classify the three examples below.

Singling out means isolating some or all of the records that belong to one person in a dataset, even without determining the individual’s name.

Example

A staff-survey file includes one respondent whose title is "Director" in the Legal department. That record can be isolated even though no name appears.

How to check: count the records that share each combination of quasi-identifiers. Any combination held by only one record, or by very few, is a singling-out risk.

How to reduce it: generalize or suppress quasi-identifiers until every combination meets an agreed minimum group size, which is what k-anonymity measures (Screen 10).

Linkability means connecting at least two records about the same person or group, within one dataset or across datasets. The Massachusetts and @PierreDelecto cases on Screen 05 both turned on linkage.

Example

A released loyalty-card file and a public review site both show purchase dates and store locations. Matching the two connects shoppers identified only by card number to reviewer profiles that show their names.

How to check: list the external datasets a plausible recipient might hold, then identify the attributes each shares with the release.

How to reduce it: remove or coarsen the shared attributes, avoid assigning the same codes to individuals in different releases, and record the linkage assessment with the release decision.

Inference means deducing, with significant probability, the value of an attribute from the values of other attributes. A release can pass singling-out and linkability checks and still allow it.

Example

Every employee in a released "Finance, age 30–39" group has the same benefits-claim code. Knowing a colleague is in that group reveals the code, though no single record was isolated.

How to check: for each group of records sharing quasi-identifiers, look at how the sensitive values are spread. If most records in a group share the same value, that value can be inferred for anyone known to be in the group.

How to reduce it: require variety in sensitive values within each group, which is what l-diversity measures (Screen 11), or, for published statistics, use differential privacy, a method that adds calibrated random variation so that results reveal little about any one individual.

At level 3, one class of three women aged 60–69 falls short; suppressing those three records meets the threshold but removes them from any analysis. At level 4, removing sex as a quasi-identifier merges the classes so all of them meet k = 5 without suppression, but the data can no longer be analyzed by sex. Which route is better depends on what the recipient needs the data for.

Selecting a value for k

No single value of k is universally safe. Practice uses values such as 3, 5, or 10, and some health-data programs suppress any count below 11. The right threshold depends on the sensitivity of the data, who receives it, and the other controls in place.

Limitations of k-anonymity

K-anonymity measures only the attributes designated as quasi-identifiers. It does not address the sensitive values within a class (Screen 11), and it does not indicate whether an external dataset holds other attributes that could link records. It is an audit measure and does not guarantee anonymity.

L-diversity and sensitive-attribute disclosure

A k-anonymous release can still disclose a sensitive attribute. If every record in an equivalence class has the same diagnosis, anyone who knows that an individual is in that class can infer the diagnosis without locating the individual's record.

Machanavajjhala and colleagues (2006) proposed l-diversity: each equivalence class should contain at least l "well-represented" values of the sensitive attribute. The simplest version, distinct l-diversity, counts distinct values. Stricter versions, such as entropy l-diversity, also consider how evenly the values are spread.

Limitations of l-diversity

L-diversity does not remove all inference risk. If one value dominates a class, an observer can still guess it correctly most of the time. If the distinct values are similar, such as three different heart conditions, an observer can still infer sensitive information. Later methods address some of these gaps: t-closeness (Li and colleagues, 2007) requires each group's mix of values to resemble the dataset as a whole, and differential privacy (Dwork and colleagues, 2006) adds calibrated random variation to published results.

Scope of k-anonymity and l-diversity

K-anonymity addresses singling out within the released data. L-diversity addresses one form of inference. Neither measures linkability to external datasets or covers attributes not designated as quasi-identifiers, so a release review still needs a linkage assessment.

Apply and assess/Screen 12/Integrated workplace exercise

Data release review

The checklist below restates the module's principles as review questions. The exercise that follows applies them to a realistic proposal.

Release review checklist

  1. Purpose.Is the purpose specific, documented, and lawful? Does the recipient need record-level data to achieve it?
  2. Minimization.Is every field necessary for that purpose? Can any be removed, aggregated, or coarsened?
  3. Identifiers.Which direct identifiers and quasi-identifiers remain? Which external sources hold the same attributes?
  4. Singling out.Is every combination of quasi-identifiers shared by at least the agreed minimum number of records (k)?
  5. Inference.Do the records in any group share the same sensitive value, so that membership in the group reveals it?
  6. Recipient controls.Do the contract and technical controls limit onward sharing, re-identification attempts, retention, and reuse?
  7. Reversibility.Copies may not be recoverable once shared. Is the remaining risk acceptable and documented, with an owner and a review date?
